# Overcompletion and self-commentary

Read this before rewriting a model-drafted text, any text with notes, transcripts or research behind it, or a draft that passes the word grep and still sounds generated. These tells use plain words, so the final grep in SKILL.md rarely finds them.

Generated prose completes its source into the neatest possible version. It adds causes the source never gave, makes the examples agree, closes open questions and ends on a lesson. A specific, unfinished truth beats an elegant false one.

## With a source

When notes, transcripts, research or an earlier human draft exist, compare the draft against them before any style edit.

1. **Unsupported connective tissue.** Motives, private thoughts, scenes, times, sequences, quotes, causes, tests or outcomes that make the story hang together but appear nowhere in the source. Plausibility is not provenance. Remove the detail, ask the writer, or mark it as an inference.
2. **Inflated certainty.** An observation becomes an intention, a suspicion becomes a diagnosis, a possibility becomes a mechanism, a partial test becomes a general finding, an unfinished experiment becomes a transformation. Put back the source's level of certainty. Keep precise uncertainty and cut only empty hedges.
3. **False closure.** One cause explains every example, every anecdote supports the thesis, a taxonomy absorbs every exception, or a chronology is retold as a deliberate decision. Separate what happened from what the writer thinks it means, and let examples disagree when they do.
4. **Framework compliance.** Sections of suspiciously equal length and shape, every example given the same lesson, headings that form a neat system the body did not need, a list padded or trimmed to a satisfying number. Organize around the real order of discovery, evidence or decision.
5. **Decorative research.** A study added after the conclusion was settled, to lend authority without changing any judgment, or stretched past its scope to fit the example. Name the source, give the finding plainly with its limit, and say what it changes. Cut research that changes nothing.

## Without a source

Do not claim a detail was invented when you have only the finished text. Ask targeted questions instead: "Is this motive in the notes or inferred?", "Did you reach this conclusion, or did the draft?", "Do these examples really share one cause?"

## Argument self-commentary

The prose describes its argument instead of making it. It is the most durable tell in otherwise clean copy, and it shows up most at paragraph boundaries, where a model reaches for a transition. It needs no source, so check every draft for it.

- **Grading its own evidence.** "That reversal is the cleanest thing we found." "The good part is measurable." "The benchmarks are less charming." "Start with X, because it's the strongest evidence we have."
- **Announcing structure.** "The other half of the read is less flattering." "Then there's the map." "Verbosity is only the surface of it." "That's the pattern in miniature."
- **Labelling a claim's status.** "The open question is whether..." "Two things stay provisional." "One result complicates the picture." "Our provisional verdict is..."
- **Interpreting a quote before giving it.** "Mike put it best:" "The complaint was unanimous."

Either of two tests is enough to flag a sentence:

- **Deletion test.** Cut it. If the argument still stands and only guidance for the reader is lost, it was scaffolding.
- **Referent test.** Its subject is the document rather than the topic: the read, the section, the picture, the case, the evidence, the story, the takeaway, the boundary.

Fix it by deleting the sentence or turning it into the fact it described. "The open question is whether it scales" becomes "Forty hours isn't long enough to tell whether it scales." A turn becomes one word such as "also". A quote gets the speaker and their role, with no verdict on it.

Deciding between findings is a claim, not commentary, and stays. "Mike's construction check could change the hotel choice. The HTML page could not" stays. "The construction check is the stronger finding" goes.

## Performed voice

Asked for a voice, a model oversupplies its signature moves. Judge by how often they appear, not whether they appear at all: several jokes, metaphors, coinages, asides or fragments in one paragraph, a short punchline closing most paragraphs, quirky details with no source, or lines whose main job is to sound authored. One conspicuous voice move per paragraph is usually plenty. Keep oddities that come from the writer and cut simulated personality. Never add mess, errors or invented specifics to make the text seem human.

## Empty figures of speech

Flag a metaphor or analogy when any of these hold:

- Deleting it loses no meaning.
- The text never names what the two things share.
- Another topic's nouns could be swapped in unchanged.
- It repeats the literal sentence beside it.
- An extended metaphor keeps calling back, into headings and the ending, without adding anything new.

Worn-out images count too: "tapestry of", "treasure trove", "double-edged sword", "tip of the iceberg", "uncharted waters", "beacon of hope", "at a crossroads", "blueprint for success", "symphony of", "mosaic of". Cut the image and say the literal thing, or name the correspondence it shows. Keep a figure that explains a mechanism or makes an abstraction graspable.

## Reader assignment

"You" telling readers what they have done, felt or feared, or "we" turning one person's experience into everyone's condition. Stay with "I" while the evidence is personal, and offer recognition rather than assigning it.

## Over-resolved endings

An ending that claims a decision or change the body never showed, settles uncertainty the writer left open, turns one experience into a universal lesson, or gives every recurring image a ceremonial callback. End one step before the model wants to: on a changed decision, a cost, a consequence or a specific open question.
